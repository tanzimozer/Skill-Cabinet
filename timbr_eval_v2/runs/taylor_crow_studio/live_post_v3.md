Most studios pick one decade and build for it. Taylor Crow Studio doesn't. It builds for the whole arc.

## The studio

2717 E Union St. Madrona. One room, real equipment, no big-box floor, no rotating cast of strangers on the rack next to you. Taylor Crow opened the doors in 2022, and the origin story isn't a business plan. It's a pay dispute. A corporate customer-service job paid her sixteen dollars an hour, then fought her on hours she'd actually worked. She fought back for two months, walked, and dog-sat to cover rent. That money became the studio's first equipment order. Crow trained in martial arts from age twelve, got Zumba-certified at sixteen, and spent high school moving through sports medicine, powerlifting, CrossFit, barre, and dance fitness before UW handed her a dance degree in 2021. NASM certification came next. Her client list runs from total beginners to competitive athletes. No two programs look the same, and that's the point.

## Private, by design

Madrona sits on Seattle's east side, a short run down to Lake Washington, quiet in a way Capitol Hill and South Lake Union aren't. Taylor Crow Studio uses that quiet on purpose. Sessions run one-on-one or in small groups, capped low enough that Crow and her trainers can actually watch your form, not guess at it from across a crowded floor. No franchise scale here. No programming built for two hundred people and handed to you anyway. Just a room built around the person actually training in it.

## Training built around the whole life cycle

The programming runs by life stage. Not by template. Bone density work anchors sessions for older clients, strength training as the real defense against osteoporosis, not an afterthought tacked onto the end of a workout. Prenatal clients build muscle ahead of delivery, which lowers complication risk. Postpartum clients rebuild through a return that respects actual recovery timelines, something most franchise gyms never bother to program for. Menopausal clients train to hold onto muscle, bone density, and joint mobility, while the sessions double as a real tool against the mood swings and energy crashes that come with the transition. Four life stages. Four different programs. One studio, one coaching philosophy: anatomy and kinesiology first, template second. Bone density work alone can be the difference between a fall and a fracture for a client in her sixties. That's not marketing language. That's the actual stake.

## Aerial conditioning and in-home beginners

Past the main floor, the studio runs aerial conditioning, a specialty almost no Seattle gym touches. The work builds the shoulder stability, core strength, and flexibility that silks and rings actually demand, and it comes straight from Crow's own background as a performer and choreographer, not a certification picked up on a weekend. On the other end of the spectrum: beginner-focused in-home training, for clients who want their first rep taken somewhere lower-pressure than a studio floor. Different entry points. Same philosophy behind both. Most studios that offer aerial work bolt it onto a yoga schedule as an add-on class. Crow built it as its own discipline.

## Corporate wellness

Corporate wellness is the third track, and it's built for teams, not individuals. Group city walks combine low-impact movement with real Seattle incline and enough pace to actually talk. Group fitness classes get built for mixed ability levels inside one team, so nobody has to clear some arbitrary fitness bar before they're allowed to show up. Mobility and recovery sessions chase posture and soreness, not leaderboard numbers, one yoga mat per person, nothing else required. Inquiries get a response inside 48 hours. Programming gets built around what the team actually needs, not a package sold the same way to everyone. Same principle as the life-stage work. Just scaled to a group instead of one body.

## The team, and how they get paid

Six trainers run the floor: Crow, Carena Holmes, Juan Pablo "JP" Perez (a Doctor of Physical Therapy candidate), Meredith Trombly, Pallis Aurora, and yoga instructor Sophie Haegele. Contracted trainers work on a 90 percent commission split, well above the standard studio cut, and it's not a line on a mission-statement page. It's Crow's stated position on fair pay for female, queer, and minority fitness professionals, built directly into payroll instead of left as a talking point.
